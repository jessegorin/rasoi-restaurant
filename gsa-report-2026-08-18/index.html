Eleven days ago we sent you a review built around four search themes — catering, buffet, butter chicken, delivery — and a list of words to add to your website. That analysis was correct as far as it went. This one goes a layer deeper, into what people actually typed before your ad appeared, and it changes the priority order.

The short version. Your keywords say “butter chicken” and “indian food catering.” What people are actually typing is “rasoi,” “rasoi near me” and “rasoi edison.” Because every keyword is set to the loosest possible matching, Google is using your food keywords to charge you for people searching your restaurant by name — customers who were already coming to you.

The finding that changes the plan

Where your money actually went

Every search that cost more than 10¢ last month, sorted into three groups. This is the search-term report, not the keyword list — the difference between the two is the whole point.

On the two restaurants that are actually running, the concentration is close to total: 96.7% of Jersey City’s traced spend and 99.0% of Iselin’s went to people searching for Rasoi by name. Generic discovery searches — somebody who does not know you yet, looking for Indian food nearby — accounted for $4.30 at Jersey City and nothing at all at Iselin.

Why this matters in money terms. Somebody who types “rasoi near me” is already your customer. They would very likely have found you through the free Google map listing, or your own site, at no cost. Every one of those clicks you pay for is a customer you were probably getting anyway — so a large share of the $707.95 in orders this month is revenue you would have had without the ads.

This is not a case of Google behaving badly. It is the predictable result of two settings working together, and both are fixable.

What your name costs you

“rasoi” costs 51¢ a click. “indian restaurant near me” costs 4.5¢ — and it also produced orders.

That last number is the one to sit with. Your bidding is set to chase conversions automatically, and brand searches convert best — naturally, since those people already chose you. So the automatic bidding pours the budget into your own name at 51¢ a click, exhausts the $5 daily limit, and stops. The cheap searches from people who have never heard of you, at four and a half cents, get whatever is left. Last month that was almost nothing.

Why the wording still matters

Relevance is capping your reach, not budget

Google splits the searches you could have appeared for into three groups: the ones you won, the ones lost to a more relevant advertiser, and the ones lost because the daily budget ran out.

The picture is essentially unchanged from August 7. At Jersey City you lose 3.5 times more reach to relevance than to budget; at Iselin, 2.7 times. Raising the daily budget without fixing the wording would just buy more clicks on pages Google already rates 1 out of 10.

But note what this figure includes. A large part of the search volume you are “winning” is people typing your own name — searches you would win anyway. The genuine competitive gap, against people who have not chosen a restaurant yet, is wider than these bars suggest.

How return on ad spend is calculated. Revenue counts completed online orders attributed to the ads through Chowly online ordering only — the “Rasoi Restaurant COO Orders” conversion action. Google also records engagement actions on this account: phone calls, directions requests, menu views, website visits and store visits, each counted by Google as a conversion worth $1. Including them would inflate the return several times over, so they are excluded from every return figure here. Phone calls are reported separately as a count, not as revenue. Worked example for this period: revenue $707.95 ÷ spend $382.21 = 1.85×; the 91 phone calls counted by Google as $91 of conversion value are excluded.

A known limitation. Some genuine online orders are not credited to the ads at all, because our ordering pages do not always pass Google the tracking marker needed to connect an order back to the click. Your true return is therefore likely somewhat better than the figures here. We are working on it, and will tell you when it is fixed rather than quietly restating past numbers.

On the brand-spend figure. The 72% is calculated from the search terms Google reports, which covered $302.36 of the $382.21 spent. Google withholds very low-volume search terms for privacy, so the true brand share could be a few points either way. The direction is not in doubt: on the two live campaigns, brand searches are 96.7% and 99.0% of traced spend.
