Your question, answered

Which terms belong on the website — and why the page they go on matters

You asked for a cross-reference of your Google Ads keywords against the website. Below is that list, ranked by what each term actually earned last month rather than by how much traffic it brought. One structural point first, because it changes where the edits need to land.

Two pages

Your ads don't land on your homepage

Every ad points at your online ordering page — rasoi123.com/store/… — not the marketing site. Words added to the homepage help your search ranking. Words added to the ordering page are what Google reads when it decides how often to show your ad and what to charge you.

1–3 of 10

Your biggest keywords score badly on relevance

Google rates "indian buffet" 1 out of 10 and "butter chicken" 1–3 out of 10 for quality on your account. The main reason is that the page the click lands on doesn't use those words. That is a wording problem, not a budget problem.

52–60%

Relevance, not budget, is capping your reach

At Jersey City and Iselin you are losing 52–60% of available searches to ad rank and only 16–19% to budget. Roughly three times as much lost visibility comes from relevance as from money.

Practically, this means each recommendation below has two homes. The marketing site edit is what shows up in the monthly SEO report you track. The ordering-page edit is what lowers your cost per click. Doing only the first will improve your rankings but leave the ad costs where they are.

Why the wording matters

You are losing more reach to relevance than to budget

Google splits the searches you could have appeared for into three buckets: the ones you won, the ones you lost because a more relevant advertiser outranked you, and the ones you lost because your daily budget ran out. The split tells you which lever to pull.

Read across the top two bars and the case for the website edits makes itself. At Jersey City and Iselin, three times more reach is going to competitors on relevance than is being lost to your budget. Putting the words your customers actually search — butter chicken, catering, lunch buffet, Edison — onto the pages your ads land on is what moves that 52–60% down. Raising the daily budget without doing it just buys more expensive clicks on pages Google already rates 1 out of 10.
